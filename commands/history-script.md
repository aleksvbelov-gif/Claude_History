## STYLE GUIDE

### THE FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLE

This is literary storytelling, not journalism. Write like a skilled author who happens to know history — not like a Wikipedia article read aloud. The viewer should feel they are being told a story by someone who genuinely cares about this person and what happened to them.

Every paragraph must do one of three things: reveal something new, deepen the viewer's sense of who this person was, or advance the emotional arc of the story. Cut anything that only provides context without doing one of these.

---

### SENTENCE AND PARAGRAPH STRUCTURE

Use these patterns:

**The staccato triple** — three short declarative sentences, each landing like a punch:
> "She was not a soldier. She was not a political figure. She was a princess."

**The long setup, short landing** — build with a long sentence, cut it short for impact:
> "She ran down the stairs without a hat or coat — someone threw a cloak over her shoulders at the door — and redirected her sleigh toward the source of the sound. What she found is genuinely difficult to put into words."

**The single-sentence paragraph** — one sentence standing alone is emphasis. Use it for the most important moments.

**The list that breaks** — a list building in one direction, then the final item lands differently:
> "She loved music. She loved the theatre. She loved good food. She also loved spending money she didn't have on all of it."

Paragraph length: 1–4 sentences maximum. Short paragraphs create pace.

---

### TENSE

- **Past tense** for narration throughout
- **Present tense** for the most dramatic scenes only — it pulls the viewer in:
> "On February 18th, just after noon, his carriage leaves the Nicholas Palace. Ella is in her studio working on embroidery. Then the explosion."

Use present tense sparingly — only for one or two pivotal scenes.

---

### TONE

The tone must match the story. These are not all the same:

- A tragic martyr → austere, unflinching, reverent
- A forgotten eccentric who achieved something lasting → warm, ironic, occasionally funny
- A life that bridged centuries → elegiac, measured, awed by the span of time

Commit to the register this particular story demands. Do not write a generic history narrator voice.

Direct address to the viewer should feel like a knowledgeable friend, not a presenter:
> "What she found at the explosion site is genuinely difficult to put into words."
> "She was also, it should be noted, genuinely beautiful in other respects."
> "This is not, in the world of Victorian royal marriages, guaranteed."

Dry asides and understated observations are welcome. Over-emoting is not. Never tell the viewer how to feel — build to the emotion and let it land on its own.

---

### THE HOOK

The hook is the most important section. It must:
1. Begin *in medias res* — inside a moment, not before it
2. Create a question the viewer needs answered
3. End with a thematic reframe: *this is not just a story about [event] — it is a story about [deeper human theme]*

**Four approaches — choose the one that fits this story:**

**1. The Final Scene First** — Open at the most dramatic moment. Do not name the person yet.
> *On the night of July 17, 1918, somewhere in the Ural forest, a woman was pushed alive into a mine shaft sixty meters deep. She was fifty-three years old. She had survived a revolution, a world war, and the bomb that killed her husband in front of her eyes. She had been offered an escape route — and she had turned it down. She was not a soldier. She was not a political figure. She was a princess.*

**2. The Contradiction** — Establish what the world saw, then immediately undercut it.
> *Imagine a person who, as a child, sat on Queen Victoria's lap, and in her old age witnessed the dawn of color television and the rise of the pop group ABBA. A person who hosted Winston Churchill in her dining room, hunted in the desert with the founder of Saudi Arabia, and on Sundays… simply boarded a regular London bus, carrying her shopping bags.*

**3. The Surprising Opening Quote** — A real, verified quote that immediately establishes the central irony.
> *Queen Victoria once looked at her first cousin, sighed, and wrote in her diary: "Her size is fearful. It is really a misfortune." The cousin in question was Princess Mary Adelaide of Cambridge. She was large. She was loud. She was perpetually overdrawn. And she was, by a very wide margin, the most beloved royal in Britain after the Queen herself.*

**4. The Impossible Choice** — Open at the moment they had to choose between two lives.
> *In every royal family, there are those who stand beneath the spotlight and those who remain in the shadows — the "spares," whose lives are measured only by their proximity to the throne. He was born fourth in line to the Crown, but throughout his life he desperately tried to erase that number from his identity. To him, his title was not a gift but a wall. This is not simply the story of a prince who died in an air crash. It is a profound psychological drama about a man who finally found himself — and at that very moment, fate drew the final line.*

**Hook length:** 150–250 words. Flowing prose only — no lists, no bullet points.

---

### THE ENDING

Every script ends in three beats:

**Beat 1: Reflection** — a final thought that crystallizes what this life meant. Not a summary. One short paragraph, quiet rather than triumphant.
> *A German princess who became more Russian than most Russians. A grand duchess who chose a slum hospital over a palace. A widow who offered forgiveness to her husband's killer. A woman who, in the darkness of a mine shaft, with broken bones, found someone in worse condition and tended to his wounds. Her name was Ella. And every step of this she chose herself.*

**Beat 2: The Viewer Question** — one genuine question specific to this story. Not "what did you think?" — something the viewer will still be turning over tomorrow.
> *We talk about courage as fighting back, resisting, surviving. But what do you call a person whose form of courage was to stay, to forgive, and to sing in the dark?*
> *She was told her entire life she was unmarriageable, unsuitable, too much. She ignored all of it and built something that is still running today. What would that look like now?*

**Beat 3: The CTA** — brief and warm:
> *Thank you for watching. If this story stayed with you, subscribe and leave a like — it helps this channel reach more people. Leave your answer in the comments. I read every one. See you in the next video.*

### LANGUAGE RULES

- British/neutral English — no American slang
- Specific verified details: exact dates, real names, precise numbers — they are the anchors of credibility
- Strong verbs, concrete nouns, active voice
- Metaphors only when earned and rooted in the subject's actual world
- No stage directions, no camera notes, no parenthetical asides

---

### LENGTH AND FORMAT

**Length:** 3000–3500 words of narration text.

**Format:** Continuous flowing prose, exactly as it would be read aloud. No section headers inside the script. No [SECTION: Name] markers. No bullet points inside the narration. After the narration: the **## Sources** section.
